TL;DR — On March 30, 2026, Google and Oratomic simultaneously published papers showing that ECC-256 encryption can be broken with far fewer quantum qubits than previously expected. In response, Google and Cloudflare dramatically moved their PQC migration deadline up from 2035 to 2029.

Opening

Word is going around that the shift to post-quantum cryptography has been moved up to 2029. Google and Cloudflare had previously planned to complete their PQC transition by 2035, in line with the U.S. government's published timeline. Recently (April 2026), though, they pulled that deadline in dramatically, to 2029. What's behind this?

The Startup: Oratomic

First, you need to know about a company called Oratomic. Oratomic is a stealth startup that came out of hiding on March 30, 2026. "Stealth startup" literally means a startup that doesn't disclose what it's working on to the public — and this paper is what revealed the direction the company is actually headed. Oratomic states its mission on its homepage as follows:

At Oratomic, we are building the world's first utility-scale quantum computers, enabled by a new regime of ultra-efficient error correction — using only light and atoms.

In other words, their goal is a utility-scale quantum computer — one large enough to directly solve real-world problems — i.e., a Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computer (FTQC). Looking at Oratomic's key people, they come from Caltech, Harvard, Google Quantum AI, Amazon, and UC Berkeley — it's fair to call them some of the sharpest minds of this era.

Why This Became a Big Deal

Google and Oratomic each recently published papers showing that breaking elliptic curve cryptography requires far fewer qubits than previously assumed, and it caused a huge stir. Let's start with Google's paper.

📄 Securing Elliptic Curve Cryptocurrencies against Quantum Vulnerabilities: Resource Estimates and Mitigations ↗

This paper's conclusion
Google re-optimized the algorithm needed to break ECC-256 (Elliptic Curve Cryptography) — the core cryptography behind modern blockchains — and recalculated the resources required. The result was dramatically lower than expected:
  • Under 1,200 logical qubits + under 90 million Toffoli gates, or
  • Under 1,450 logical qubits + under 70 million Toffoli gates

Either of those is enough to break ECC. In terms of physical qubits, that comes out to under 500,000 — a 20x reduction from the 13 million qubits estimated back in 2022 (Webber et al.).

*Shor's Algorithm: a quantum computing algorithm developed in 1994 that can break both elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) and RSA (used by most banks). It's the core threat to the signature schemes used by Bitcoin and Ethereum. Computing a public key from a private key is easy, but reversing a public key back into a private key is effectively impossible on a classical computer — Shor's algorithm makes that computation feasible.

**Physical qubits: the basic unit of real hardware. They're noisy, so complex computation is impossible without error correction.
**Logical qubits: a "clean" unit made by bundling dozens to hundreds of physical qubits together with automatic error correction — this is what actual algorithms use.

Core Techniques

Here's how Google cut down the resources required for the attack.

(1) Redesigning Shor's Algorithm

The part of Shor's algorithm that repeats elliptic curve point addition consumes the most Toffoli gates. For ECC-256, Google cut the required 256 point additions down to 28, and optimized the internal arithmetic with Montgomery multiplication and "kickmix," compressing the Toffoli count by roughly 10x.

  • Before: 256 point additions × ~2.7M Toffoli gates each ≈ ~700M total
  • Redesigned (windowed arithmetic, window size = 16): 256 ÷ 16 = 16 steps × 2 registers = 28 windowed point additions
  • 28 × ~2.7M Toffoli gates ≈ ~75.6M total
  • Montgomery trick + kickmix optimization → final Toffoli count < 90M (roughly a 10x compression)

(2) Shortening Magic State Distillation

Running a Toffoli gate requires a continuous supply of a special quantum state called a "T state." Producing it has historically consumed most of a quantum computer's resources. Because computing with a "dirty" T state gives wrong answers, you have to run a factory that purifies dirty T states into clean ones — and that process wastes so many T states (anywhere from 15:1 to 225:1) that most of the machine ends up dedicated to the factory. Google parallelized and densified this factory to remove the bottleneck in producing clean T states.

  • Before (1-level distillation): 15 dirty T states → 1 clean T state (15:1)
  • Before (2-level distillation): 225 dirty T states → 1 clean T state (225:1) → ~80–90% of the machine's resources spent on the factory
  • Google's improvement: densely packing the factory with a "yoked surface code," plus reaction-limited execution to minimize idle time → the same physical qubits now produce more T states in parallel

(3) Shor's Algorithm Priming

Running Shor's algorithm breaks down into two stages.

This is where the concept of "priming" comes in: run stage 1 ahead of time and keep it "loaded," then execute only stage 2 the moment a public key is exposed. By finishing the prep stage and waiting, an attacker can start reversing a private key the instant an individual public key hits the mempool — cutting the time required roughly in half.

  • Without priming: public key exposed → stage 1 (~9–12 min) + stage 2 = ~18–23 min total → exceeds block time (~10 min) → on-spend attack not feasible
  • With priming: [beforehand] stage 1 already complete → [the moment the key is exposed] only stage 2 runs → ~9 min → on-spend attack succeeds with ~41% probability
  • Additional optimization via state reuse: reusing stage-1 state lets a single prep attack up to 9 public keys in a row
Notable
Google didn't actually implement any of this — they only produced the design and completed the calculations. Anticipating that bad actors could exploit it, they decided, after discussions with the U.S. government, not to release the design itself. Instead, they published only a zero-knowledge proof of the calculation's correctness.

Also, the paper's design calls for 1,200 logical qubits, while the largest number of logical qubits humanity has actually built so far is 48 (as of 2023) — so this isn't imminent. But it's a warning that far fewer resources are needed than previously thought, so preparation needs to start now.

Why This Matters

ECDSA elliptic curve cryptography is used across a huge range of existing cryptographic systems — from personal certificates to the smart contracts behind cryptocurrencies. Its security rests entirely on the assumption that solving the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP) is effectively impossible.

If that assumption falls apart as quantum computers advance, a large amount of intangible financial assets could vanish from people's wallets in an instant.

Most blockchains, including Bitcoin and Ethereum, use secp256k1-based ECDSA to sign transactions — so this isn't a purely theoretical threat. An attacker could intercept transactions, hijack administrative control of a smart contract, or arbitrarily move funds out of someone else's wallet.

This Isn't the End

The Oratomic paper mentioned earlier takes this one step further.

📄 Shor's algorithm is possible with as few as 10,000 reconfigurable atomic qubits ↗

This paper's conclusion
Just 26,000 physical qubits are enough to break ECC-256 within a matter of days.

Oratomic took Google's physical-layer optimizations to Shor's algorithm as a given, and layered on top of it a neutral-atom-based physical layer optimization.

Oratomic's Core Logic

(1) qLDPC (Quantum Low-Density Parity-Check) Codes

This is the new error-correction scheme Oratomic proposes.

First, a bit of background on qubits: they're extremely unstable, and even a slight interaction with the outside world — temperature, vibration, electromagnetic waves — can corrupt a qubit's state. Since computing with a corrupted state gives the wrong answer, qubit errors have to be corrected.

Classical computers correct errors by simply copying data across multiple bits and taking the majority value when checking. Quantum computers can't do that — the laws of quantum mechanics don't allow you to copy the original bit's data. So instead of copying, a quantum computer spreads a value across multiple qubits.

*A quirk of quantum mechanics: a qubit's defining feature is superposition — it holds a state that is both 0 and 1 at once, but the moment you read it, it collapses to either 0 or 1 and the original value is lost. Because the "original" you'd want to copy disappears the instant you read it, it simply cannot be copied.

Error correction on a quantum computer works via a method called the surface code: an auxiliary physical qubit (an "ancilla") is placed somewhere it can become entangled with a given physical qubit, and measuring that relationship reveals whether something changed — without ever directly measuring the physical qubit itself. But in the surface code, the physical layer is arranged so an ancilla can only entangle with its immediate neighbors, meaning a single ancilla can verify at most 4 physical qubits. As a result, the number of physical qubits actually usable for computation dropped sharply.

Google's paper, discussed above, also uses the widely-adopted surface code — and it ends up spending 97% of its physical qubits on error correction, leaving only 3% available for actual computation.

Oratomic's proposal uses optical tweezers — a highly focused laser beam — to physically pick up neutral atoms and let an ancilla entangle with physical qubits that aren't its immediate neighbors. That lets a single ancilla check the error state of 6 to 12 physical qubits instead of 4. It's harder to implement than the surface code, but it drops the number of physical qubits required from Google's 500,000 down to 26,000 — roughly a 20x reduction.

Oratomic's approach has its own weakness, of course — breaking ECC-256 with this architecture takes 10 days.

Why Oratomic's Paper Is a Problem

Because of that, what Oratomic's research really targets isn't an on-spend attack, but an at-rest attack — one against funds that are already sitting exposed. According to Oratomic, roughly 6.7 million bitcoins are already vulnerable to a quantum attack today, meaning their public keys are already exposed.

And the largest neutral-atom quantum computer in the world already has 6,100 qubits. It used to be said that millions of qubits would be needed — in ten years, the number required has dropped by more than 30,000x. The drop over just the last year has been the steepest, and the most alarming. 2029 is the target date, but there's no telling what quantum computing breakthrough might happen before then.
